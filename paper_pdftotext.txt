communication improves collective reasoning.
We investigate a complementary hypothesis:
that communication topology may matter more
than communication volume. To study this

patterns among debating agents under an information bottleneck objective.

Adaptive Communication for Collective
Reasoning

Our objective is to study the relative contributions
of communication topology and communication
volume to collective reasoning. To do so, we construct an adaptive communication framework that
allows both factors to be manipulated independently. The framework consists of three components: (1) a communication topology controller
that determines which agents exchange information, (2) a communication bottleneck that controls
how much information is transmitted, and (3) a debate process in which agents iteratively revise their
answers using received information.
This design enables controlled experiments in
which communication structure and communication bandwidth can be varied separately, allowing
us to isolate their effects on collective reasoning
performance.

S PARSE C OMM maintains a pool of N = 3 agents
with distinct reasoning styles (analytical, step-bystep, and creative). At round 0, all agents independently generate answers and reasoning traces.
During each subsequent round, a communication
controller observes the current debate state, generates a weighted communication graph Wt , and
determines whether additional communication is
likely to improve collective performance.
The communication graph governs both who
communicates with whom (topology) and how
much information is transmitted (volume). Agents

Partial Communication Emerges
Universally

Across all datasets and all three evaluation seeds,
the learned controller overwhelmingly favors partial communication. Active edges consistently operate in the intermediate communication regime,
while neither complete communication nor complete suppression emerges as a preferred strategy.
This behavior is noteworthy because unrestricted
debate corresponds to transmitting every generated

token, whereas no-communication baselines eliminate information exchange entirely. The learned
controller converges to neither extreme.
Instead, the system consistently adopts selective
information sharing, suggesting that effective collective reasoning relies on transmitting a carefully
chosen subset of information rather than maximizing or minimizing communication.
Furthermore, communication density and compression ratio exhibit a strong positive correlation
(r = 0.917, p < 10−40 ), indicating that topology
and bandwidth are tightly coupled. When the controller increases connectivity, it simultaneously allocates additional communication capacity to those
connections. This supports the view that communication structure and communication volume should
be studied jointly rather than as independent design
choices.

While our results provide evidence that communication topology is an important factor in collective
LLM reasoning, several limitations remain.

490

Scale of training. The communication controllers are trained using relatively small amounts
of task-specific experience. Although the learned
policies transfer across evaluation seeds and consistently recover sparse communication structures,
larger-scale training may reveal additional communication regimes or more robust policies. Future work should investigate whether the observed
topological effects persist under substantially larger
training budgets.

493

Model-family diversity. All experiments use the
same underlying language model backend. Consequently, our conclusions isolate the effect of communication structure within a single model family
but do not establish whether identical communication patterns emerge across architectures. Evaluating topology learning on open-weight and frontier
models would strengthen the generality of the findings.

503

representative of many contemporary debate frameworks, larger agent societies may exhibit qualitatively different communication dynamics, including hierarchical organization, specialization, or
coalition formation. Understanding how communication topology scales with group size remains an

Conclusion

7

More importantly, the experiments reveal three
broader insights about collective reasoning. First,
communication topology exerts a larger influence
on performance than communication volume: factorial analysis shows that adaptive routing contributes approximately twice the performance gain
of communication sparsification alone. Second,
successful reasoning episodes are associated with
asymmetric communication structures, suggesting
that effective collaboration emerges through temporary specialization rather than uniform information exchange. Third, communication requirements
vary dramatically across tasks, revealing a spectrum of reasoning compressibility ranging from
near-silent coordination in mathematical problem
solving to substantially higher bandwidth requirements in multi-hop factual retrieval.
Taken together, these findings suggest that communication should not be viewed solely as a resource to be minimized. Instead, communication structure itself appears to be a fundamental
component of collective intelligence in languagemodel societies. As multi-agent systems continue
to scale, understanding how information flows
through groups may prove as important as understanding the capabilities of the individual agents
themselves.
